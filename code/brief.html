County achievement gaps recovered from proficiency counts

Districts report how many students took the state test and what share met the standard, and every state sets its own test. This page recovers from that thin record the gap between White and Black students' average mathematics achievement in <> counties, and checks the result against an independent archive.

By Felipe Hernán Rodríguez Villalta, 2026 · EDFacts, Common Core of Data and SAIPE through the Urban Institute Education Data Portal · Stata and Mata; validated against SEDA 6.0

The federal record of state testing is public, complete and coarse. For every district, grade and student group, EDFacts publishes the number of students tested and the share who scored at or above the state's proficient standard, in ranges when the group is small. It publishes no scores, and each state tests with its own instrument against its own standard. A difference of ten percentage points in proficiency means one thing in a state with a demanding standard and another in a state with a lenient one, and the same difference means different things at the top and at the bottom of the distribution (Ho 2008).

This page reports what can nonetheless be learned from that record about the gap between White and Black students in mathematics, for the counties of Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi, North Carolina and South Carolina in school year 2018-19. The gaps are expressed in standard deviation units of student achievement, a metric that does not depend on the state's test or standard, so they can be placed side by side across states. The page describes differences in measured achievement between groups of children who go to school in very different conditions. It does not attribute those differences to the children, and it does not identify their causes.

Chapter 1Counts by category, not scores

States report test results to the federal government as counts of students in ordered performance categories. The categories carry real information about where a group's scores lie, but the information is coarsened: a student at the bottom of "proficient" and one at the top are the same student in the count. Ho and Reardon (2012) showed how to estimate the gap between two groups from such counts, and Reardon, Shear, Castellano and Ho (2017) how to recover each group's mean and standard deviation. Their model, a heteroskedastic ordered probit, treats the categories as cuts through a continuous distribution and estimates where each group's distribution sits relative to cut scores shared by the groups in the fit, here the three groups of a county and grade.

The public files add a second coarsening. They publish one cut, the proficient standard, not the full set of categories. The pipeline behind this page therefore reconstructs four categories from each group's proficiency rate under two stated assumptions: scores within a group are normally distributed with the same spread in every group, and the state's cut scores sit at fixed positions around the proficient standard. The assumptions fix the spread; what the data identify is where each group sits relative to the standard, and so the distance between groups. The gap is summarized as Ho's (2009) V, which is unchanged by any increasing transformation of a state's scale and which, under the normality assumed here, equals the difference in means divided by the pooled standard deviation.

The gap is estimated for every county and grade, then the three grades of a county are pooled into one estimate with weights that favor the more precise grades, following the meta-analytic model of Hedges and Vevea (1998). District cells with fewer than <> tested students are set aside before aggregation, because the published rates for small groups are too coarse; this rule excludes <>% of the Black students tested in the six states. Counties then enter only when at least <> Black students were tested in each grade, which keeps <> of the <> counties and a further <>% of the remaining Black test takers outside the sample. In <> of those counties no district had <> White test takers in any grade, so a gap is estimated for <> counties.

Chapter 3Gaps and the conditions around them

Racial achievement gaps are closely associated with racial differences in family resources, in the poverty of the schools students attend and in segregation (Reardon, Kalogrides and Shores 2019); income gaps show the same pattern (Reardon 2011). Two county characteristics available for every county are used here to place the gaps in context: the child poverty rate of the county, from the Census Bureau's small area estimates, and the structure of its school districts.

Across these counties the child poverty rate is at most weakly associated with the size of the gap. Counties with high child poverty rates have gaps as large or as small as counties with low rates. The structure of districts is more closely associated with the gap. Counties with more school districts have larger gaps: <> standard deviations per log unit (standard error <>), or about <> for a doubling of the number of districts, with poverty, the Black share of enrollment and the unevenness of that share across districts held constant. Together the four characteristics account for <>% of the variation in county gaps.

The count of districts admits more than one reading, and these data do not distinguish between them. In these states a county with several districts may be a county system beside one or more city systems, or, in North Carolina and Louisiana especially, a county whose charter schools are each counted as a district in the federal directory; the count therefore mixes institutional fragmentation with metropolitan size. Larger racial segregation between schools is associated with larger gaps (Reardon 2016; Reardon, Weathers, Fahle, Jang and Kalogrides 2024), but these data cannot say whether the larger gaps of metropolitan counties reflect more advantaged White students, more segregated schools, or both. The measure of how unevenly Black students are spread across a county's districts adds nothing once the number of districts is in the model, and it is zero by construction in the <> single-district counties, where sorting happens between schools inside the district and is invisible in a district-level file.

Chapter 4How the estimates are checked

The Stanford Education Data Archive (SEDA) estimates county means and gaps from the same federal collection, with the full set of performance categories, a different model specification and a link to the national NAEP scale. Its sixth release covers school year 2018-19 and provides an independent set of estimates against which this pipeline's can be judged. Levels cannot be compared: SEDA's scale is national, while this pipeline's gaps are in within-county standard deviation units and its validation means are standardized within each state and grade. Within a state and grade, however, SEDA's values are a linear transformation of the state-standardized ones, so the ordering of counties and the linear association can be compared, through correlations computed within states.

About this pageData, methods and protection

Methods in brief

Data
EDFacts mathematics assessment results by district, grade and race (students tested and the midpoint of the published proficiency range), the Common Core of Data district directory and enrollment by race, and the Census Bureau's Small Area Income and Poverty Estimates for school districts, all for school year 2018-19 and all retrieved from the Urban Institute Education Data Portal with the educationdata Stata package. <>
Sample
District-grade-subgroup cells with fewer than <> tested students are excluded before aggregation (<>% of Black test takers); cells suppressed by the Department of Education (<> rows, all small) are dropped and never reconstructed. A county enters when it has a Black cell of at least <> tested students in every grade: <> counties, of which <> have a White cell of that size in at least one grade, and so a gap estimate, and <> in all three grades.
Estimation
Heteroskedastic ordered probit (Reardon, Shear, Castellano and Ho 2017) with the hetop command, one fit per county and grade with three groups (all students, White, Black) and four categories reconstructed from the proficiency rate; <> Gap V (Ho 2009) and its delta-method standard error for every cell in one Mata operation; the companion command gapci does the same for one pair of groups.
Pooling and models
Grade gaps pooled per county with a precision-weighted random-intercept model (Hedges and Vevea 1998), evaluated at grade <>. Four nested precision-weighted regressions of the pooled gap on child poverty, log number of districts, Black share of enrollment and between-district dispersion of that share, with robust standard errors.
Validation
Within-state HETOP refit across counties for each state, grade and group, correlated with SEDA 6.0 county means and White-Black gaps (cohort-standardized scale) after standardization within state and grade; and the direct probit-difference estimator with binomial standard errors as an internal check.
Protection
All sources are public and aggregated. Suppressed cells are never filled in or recovered by subtraction; no estimate on this page or in the tables rests on a cell with fewer than <> tested students; subgroup estimates appear at the county level only; the raw downloads and the SEDA file are not redistributed, and the validation figure is published as an image without the underlying values. The repository's README and manual state these rules and account for every excluded cell.
